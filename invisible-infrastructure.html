Source 1. Andrew Blum, TED talk

The internet is physically real

After a squirrel chewed through a cable outside his apartment and killed his connection, journalist Andrew Blum went looking for the actual, physical internet. He found it.

“When you see these guys going at this cable with a hacksaw, you stop thinking about the Internet as a cloud. It starts to seem like an incredibly physical thing.”

Andrew Blum, TED 2012

Data centers

Warehouse-scale buildings, often in remote, cold locations, full of servers handling billions of requests per second.

Internet exchange points

Buildings where the routers of different networks physically connect through yellow fiber-optic jumper cables.

Undersea cables

Fiber-optic lines on the ocean floor connecting continents. Surprisingly thin, extraordinarily important.

Source 2. David D. Clark, SIGCOMM 1988 (reprinted in CCR, Jan. 1995)

Why the internet was built to survive

Clark's paper captures the reasoning behind TCP/IP. It was written not as a specification but as a look back at decisions already made. The architecture flows from a single ranked list of priorities, and as Clark puts it, “an entirely different network architecture would result if the order were changed.”

  1. Goal 1

    Survivability first. Communication must continue despite loss of networks or gateways. State is kept at the endpoints, not in the network. Clark calls this fate-sharing, meaning it is acceptable to lose state only if the endpoint itself is also lost.

  2. Goal 2

    Multiple types of service. TCP and IP were originally one protocol. They were split because different applications need different trade-offs between reliability, latency, and bandwidth. The datagram is a flexible building block, not a service in itself.

  3. Goal 3

    Accommodate a variety of networks. The only assumption is that the network can carry a packet of reasonable size with imperfect reliability. That is why TCP/IP runs over Ethernet, satellite, packet radio, serial links, and undersea fiber.

  4. Goal 7 (last)

    Accountability. “During wartime, one is less concerned with detailed accounting of resources used than with mustering whatever resources are available.” This low priority still haunts internet governance today.

Grounded in Clark 1988 and the TCP/IP architecture

The internet protocol stack

  1. 5

    Application

    HTTP, HTTPS, DNS, SMTP, FTP, SSH

    What the user sees. Protocols that define how applications communicate, such as a browser, an email client, or a streaming service.

  2. 4

    Transport

    TCP, UDP

    Clark's key split. TCP gives reliable, ordered delivery (websites, files). UDP gives fast, lossy delivery (video calls, DNS). Both sit on top of the datagram.

  3. 3

    Network (IP)

    IPv4, IPv6, ICMP, BGP

    The datagram. Each packet is addressed and routed independently through gateways. This is Clark's core insight, and it is what traceroute probes hop by hop.

  4. 2

    Data link

    Ethernet, Wi-Fi (802.11), PPP

    Node-to-node delivery on a single network segment. Handles MAC addressing, framing, and error detection between neighboring devices.

  5. 1

    Physical

    Fiber, copper, radio, satellite

    Blum's world. The actual cables, including hair-thin glass fibers in 9,000-mile undersea cables, that carry pulses of light encoding every bit.

“The Internet architecture achieves this flexibility by making a minimum set of assumptions about the function which the net will provide. The basic assumption is that the network can transport a packet or datagram.”

Clark, §6 Varieties of Networks

Clark's key design move

Why IP and TCP were separated

Splitting them let IP be the minimal, universal substrate, while TCP added reliability only for those who needed it. UDP proves the point. Sometimes you don't.

The stack in the real world

Each layer maps to something physical or political

Layer 1 is Blum's undersea cables. Layer 3 is the routes Reuters' Team Telecom wants to block. Layer 4 is the reliability Clark debated. The stack is not just technical. It is geopolitical.

Source 3. NordVPN, What is traceroute?

Every packet leaves a trail

Traceroute is a network diagnostic tool that maps every intermediate hop a packet makes on its journey. Each hop is a physical router, often in a specific city, revealing exactly which real-world infrastructure your data crosses.

It makes Clark's abstract datagram model visible. Packets really do travel independently, hop by hop, through physical nodes, the very nodes Blum photographed on his travels.

Source 4. Reuters, the U.S.–China subsea cable fight

The physical internet is a battlefield

Reuters' investigation showed that the abstract internet has become a geopolitical flashpoint. The U.S. government's “Team Telecom” works to block any undersea cable that could connect U.S. territory to mainland China or Hong Kong, citing espionage risks.

China, meanwhile, has invested hundreds of millions in its own undersea cable network through state-owned firms and the Digital Silk Road, a direct counter to Western cable dominance.

“Undersea cables have become a weapon of war in this new Cold War between China and the U.S.”

Joe Brock, Reuters reporter, 2023

The infrastructure

About 560 active cable systems

Carrying roughly 99% of international internet traffic, across nearly 1.4 million km of cable on the ocean floor.

The U.S. response

Team Telecom's veto

The U.S. blocked the Pacific Light Cable Network to Hong Kong. Meta and Google had to reroute their Echo and Bifrost cables to avoid the South China Sea.

The vulnerability

Accidental and intentional cuts

Ship anchors, fishing trawlers, and possibly state-sponsored sabotage can sever cables that carry entire nations' internet traffic.

Connecting the sources

One internet, four perspectives

Together the four sources make one argument. The internet was designed to be resilient, built from glass and steel, made navigable through diagnostics, and is now contested as sovereign territory.

Clark → Blum

Protocol becomes infrastructure

Clark's datagram design (stateless gateways, endpoints holding their own state, packets routed independently) is exactly what Blum photographed. The fiber, exchange points, and landing stations carry out that philosophy at planetary scale.

Blum → Traceroute

Infrastructure becomes visible

Traceroute is a window into Blum's physical world. Each hop in a trace is a real node, a router in a rack in a building in a city, that Blum could walk into and touch.

Traceroute → Reuters

Visibility reveals vulnerability

When a trace crosses an undersea segment, it exposes the chokepoints Reuters investigated. The jumps in latency correspond to cables that states now treat as weapons.

Reuters → Clark

Survivability meets geopolitics

Clark ranked accountability last, noting that “the problem received very little attention during the early stages of the design.” That gap now defines the crisis. Undersea cables are owned by private consortia, governed by no single authority, and increasingly weaponized by states.

The central contradiction

Designed for openness, contested for control

  1. 1973

    Cerf and Kahn envision TCP/IP, a protocol to interconnect any network, designed to survive war.

  2. 1988

    Clark's SIGCOMM paper. “Survivability was put as a first goal, and accountability as a last goal.” It explains after the fact why TCP/IP is built the way it is, from ranked military priorities rather than specs.

  3. 2012

    Blum's TED talk. The cloud is actually a network of buildings, copper, and fiber. The internet has a physical address.

  4. 2023

    Reuters investigation. The physical internet is a geopolitical theater, with the U.S. and China waging a cold war on the ocean floor.

The core tension

Global architecture, national control

Clark built an architecture that crosses borders by design. Reuters shows nations now drawing borders through it, underwater.

The traceroute lesson

Every path is political

A traceroute from New York to Singapore reveals which cables your data crosses. Each of those cables is now a diplomatic negotiation, a security risk, and a potential target.

The Blum insight

Invisibility means vulnerability

Because we treat the internet as abstract, we ignore its physical stakes. If a squirrel can kill your internet, a state actor certainly can.

Conclusion

The internet is infrastructure, philosophy, and geopolitics at once

Clark gave us the rules. Blum showed us the body. Traceroute lets us follow the connection. And Reuters revealed that other powers are watching the same veins and deciding whether to cut them.
